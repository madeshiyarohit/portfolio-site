When a store isn't selling, the homepage usually gets the blame. But most buying decisions happen on the **product page**. That's where a visitor stops browsing and starts asking, *is this right for me?*

Here's how I structure a product page, from the top of the screen down.

## Above the fold: the five-second test

On a phone, the first screen should answer the basics without any scrolling:

1. **What is it?** A clear product title. Not a clever one.
2. **What does it look like?** A sharp main photo that shows the product properly.
3. **How much?** The price, plus any sale price or "from" price.
4. **Can I get it in my size/colour?** Visible variant options.
5. **How do I buy it?** An Add to Cart button that's easy to see and tap.

If any of these is missing or pushed down the page, people leave before they've really looked.

## Photos that answer questions

Good product photography isn't about having lots of images. It's about having the *right* ones:

- the product on its own, clean and well lit,
- the product in use, so people can picture owning it,
- a photo that shows **scale**: in a hand, on a person or next to something familiar,
- close-ups of texture, material or details that matter,
- every colour option, so nobody has to guess.

## The description: benefits first, details second

Most descriptions list features. The ones that sell explain why those features matter.

> **Feature:** Double-walled stainless steel.
> **Benefit:** Your coffee stays hot through the whole morning commute.

I like to start with two or three short benefit lines, then put the detailed specifications (materials, dimensions, care instructions) in tidy expandable sections below. Shoppers who want details can find them; shoppers who don't aren't overwhelmed.

## Answer doubts before they become objections

Every shopper has a few silent worries. Answer them right below the Add to Cart button:

- **When will it arrive?** An estimated delivery time.
- **What if it doesn't fit or I don't like it?** A short, friendly returns line.
- **Is my payment safe?** The payment methods you accept.
- **Is this brand legit?** Reviews, ideally with real customer photos.

These few lines often do more for sales than any redesign.

## Reviews that feel real

Five stars from "John D." doesn't build much trust. A handful of genuine reviews with names, photos and specific comments ("runs slightly large, I'd size down") is far more convincing, even if a few of them aren't perfect.

## Make the mobile experience effortless

Most of your visitors are on a phone. On mobile, I check that:

- the Add to Cart button stays easy to reach as people scroll (a sticky button helps),
- variant selectors are large enough to tap accurately,
- photos can be swiped and zoomed,
- nothing jumps around while the page loads.

## Don't forget the quiet details

- A clear "out of stock" state, ideally with a "notify me" option.
- Related products that genuinely relate.
- A page title and description written for Google, not left as the default.

## A quick self-check

Open one of your product pages on your phone and pretend you've never heard of your brand. Would you trust it enough to pay? If you hesitate, that hesitation is exactly what your customers feel too.
